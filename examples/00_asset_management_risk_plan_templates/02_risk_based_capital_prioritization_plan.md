# 1. Executive Summary

This Risk-Based Infrastructure Capital Prioritization Plan provides [Municipality / Utility Name] with a systematic framework for converting infrastructure asset risk information into actionable capital investment priorities.

The Plan evaluates infrastructure needs based on:

* Probability of Failure (PoF);
* Consequence of Failure (CoF);
* Existing asset risk;
* System criticality;
* Risk reduction achieved by proposed improvements;
* Project cost;
* Life-cycle considerations;
* Regulatory and operational requirements;
* Project readiness; and
* Available capital funding.

The objective is not simply to identify assets with the highest risk scores. The objective is to determine which investments provide the greatest benefit to the Municipality while maintaining acceptable levels of infrastructure risk.

The framework establishes the following decision process:

**Asset Condition → Asset Risk → System Risk → Mitigation Alternatives → Risk Reduction → Capital Prioritization**

Risk is initially characterized as:

$$
R_i = PoF_i \times CoF_i
$$

where:

* $R_i$ = risk associated with asset $i$;
* $PoF_i$ = probability of failure score; and
* $CoF_i$ = consequence of failure score.

For proposed improvements, the reduction in risk is estimated as:

$$
\Delta R_i = R_{Existing,i} - R_{PostProject,i}
$$

Capital investments may then be compared based on the risk reduction achieved relative to the required investment.

# 2. Purpose

The purpose of this Plan is to establish a transparent and repeatable methodology for prioritizing infrastructure investments.

The Plan is intended to support:

1. Annual capital budgeting;
2. Five-year and ten-year Capital Improvement Programs;
3. Asset renewal and replacement planning;
4. Grant and funding applications;
5. Long-term financial planning;
6. Regulatory compliance planning;
7. Infrastructure resilience planning; and
8. Communication of infrastructure priorities to elected officials and the public.

The methodology is intended to supplement, rather than replace, engineering judgment and municipal decision-making.

---

# 3. Capital Planning Objectives

The Municipality's infrastructure investment program should seek to balance the following objectives:

* Reduce unacceptable infrastructure risk;
* Maintain reliable service;
* Protect public health and safety;
* Maintain regulatory compliance;
* Protect the environment;
* Reduce emergency repair costs;
* Improve system resilience;
* Coordinate infrastructure investments;
* Maintain affordable utility rates; and
* Maximize the value obtained from available capital funding.

# 4. Risk-Based Capital Planning Framework

The capital prioritization framework consists of six primary steps.

## Step 1 – Identify Asset Risk

Determine the existing risk associated with infrastructure assets.

$$
R_{Existing} = PoF \times CoF
$$

---

## Step 2 – Identify Risk Drivers

Determine why the asset represents a risk.

Typical risk drivers include:

* Poor condition;
* Repeated failures;
* Insufficient capacity;
* Lack of redundancy;
* Obsolete equipment;
* Regulatory deficiencies;
* Flood exposure;
* Critical customers;
* Difficult emergency access;
* Long repair duration; and
* Significant environmental consequences.

Understanding the risk driver is necessary before selecting an appropriate mitigation strategy.

---

## Step 3 – Develop Mitigation Alternatives

Potential mitigation strategies should be evaluated before assuming that complete asset replacement is required.

Alternatives may include:

* Increased inspection;
* Preventive maintenance;
* Operational modifications;
* Targeted repair;
* Rehabilitation;
* Redundancy improvements;
* Emergency bypass capability;
* Flood protection;
* Partial replacement;
* Full replacement; or
* Acceptance of the existing risk.

---

## Step 4 – Estimate Post-Project Risk

For each feasible alternative, estimate the residual risk following implementation.

$$
R_{Post} = PoF_{Post} \times CoF_{Post}
$$

The improvement may reduce:

* Probability of failure;
* Consequence of failure; or
* Both.

For example, replacing a deteriorated force main may primarily reduce PoF, while installing a redundant force main may substantially reduce CoF even if the existing pipe remains in service.

---

## Step 5 – Calculate Risk Reduction

The estimated risk reduction is:

$$
\Delta R = R_{Existing} - R_{Post}
$$

The percentage reduction may also be calculated:

$$
%\ Risk\ Reduction =
\frac{R_{Existing} - R_{Post}}
{R_{Existing}}
\times 100
$$

---

## Step 6 – Prioritize Capital Investment

Projects should then be compared using risk reduction, cost, regulatory requirements, system benefits, and other municipal priorities.

# 8. Cost-Effectiveness of Risk Reduction

Capital cost should be considered in conjunction with risk reduction.

A simple risk reduction efficiency metric may be calculated as:

$$
RRE =
\frac{\Delta R}{Project\ Cost}
$$

Because project costs may be large, the Municipality may normalize cost in millions of dollars:

$$
RRE =
\frac{\Delta R}
{Project\ Cost\ ($M)}
$$

Example:

A $2.0 million project reducing risk from 20 to 5 provides:

$$
\Delta R = 20 - 5 = 15
$$

Therefore:

$$
RRE =
\frac{15}{2.0}
==============

7.5
$$

The project provides approximately **7.5 risk-reduction units per $1 million invested**.

This metric should be used as a comparative planning tool rather than an absolute economic measure.

# 9. Life-Cycle Cost Evaluation

For major projects, life-cycle cost should be considered rather than capital cost alone.

Potential costs include:

* Planning and engineering;
* Construction;
* Operations;
* Energy;
* Routine maintenance;
* Major maintenance;
* Rehabilitation;
* Emergency repair;
* Replacement; and
* Disposal or decommissioning.

The present value of life-cycle cost may be calculated as:

$$
PV =
\sum_{t=0}^{n}
\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}
$$

where:

* $C_t$ = cost occurring in year $t$;
* $r$ = discount rate; and
* $n$ = analysis period.

Risk reduction efficiency may then be evaluated using life-cycle cost:

$$
RRE_{LCC} =
\frac{\Delta R}
{PV(Life\ Cycle\ Cost)}
$$

# 10. Expected Annual Loss

For assets where sufficient information is available, the Municipality may supplement qualitative risk scoring with an economic risk estimate.

Expected Annual Loss may be expressed as:

$$
EAL = P(Failure) \times C(Failure)
$$

where:

* $P(Failure)$ = estimated annual probability of failure; and
* $C(Failure)$ = estimated monetary consequence of failure.

Potential failure costs may include:

* Emergency repairs;
* Property damage;
* Environmental remediation;
* Regulatory penalties;
* Service interruption;
* Lost revenue;
* Traffic impacts; and
* Business interruption.

For example:

If an asset has:

$$
P(Failure) = 0.08
$$

and estimated failure consequences of:

$$
C(Failure) = $2,500,000
$$

then:

$$
EAL = 0.08 \times $2,500,000
$$

$$
EAL = $200,000/year
$$

This provides an economic measure of the risk carried by continuing to operate the asset.

---

# 11. Benefit of Risk Reduction

Where Expected Annual Loss can be estimated, the economic benefit of a project may be evaluated.

$$
Annual\ Risk\ Reduction =
EAL_{Existing} - EAL_{Post}
$$

For example:

$$
EAL_{Existing} = $200,000/year
$$

$$
EAL_{Post} = $25,000/year
$$

Therefore:

$$
Annual\ Risk\ Reduction =
$175,000/year
$$

This value can be incorporated into traditional economic evaluations such as:

* Net Present Value;
* Benefit-Cost Ratio;
* Internal Rate of Return; and
* Payback Period.

# 12. Multi-Criteria Project Prioritization

Risk reduction should generally represent a major component of capital prioritization, but it should not necessarily be the only consideration.

An example weighted prioritization model is shown below.

| Criterion                | Example Weight |
| ------------------------ | -------------: |
| Risk Reduction           |            35% |
| Regulatory / Compliance  |            20% |
| Public Health & Safety   |            15% |
| Financial Efficiency     |            10% |
| Resilience / Redundancy  |            10% |
| Project Readiness        |             5% |
| Coordination Opportunity |             5% |
| **Total**                |       **100%** |

Each project may be scored from 1 to 5 for each criterion.

The weighted project score is:

$$
Priority\ Score =
\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i s_i
$$

where:

* $w_i$ = weighting assigned to criterion $i$; and
* $s_i$ = project score for criterion $i$.

The weights should be established collaboratively with municipal leadership and should reflect the Municipality's actual priorities.

# 13. Regulatory and Mandatory Projects

Certain projects may require implementation regardless of calculated risk or economic efficiency.

These may include:

* Consent order requirements;
* Permit compliance projects;
* Regulatory deadlines;
* Immediate public health hazards;
* Emergency repairs;
* Legally committed projects; and
* Contractual obligations.

These projects should be identified as **Mandatory** rather than artificially manipulating risk scores to force them to the top of the ranking.

# 20. Performance Measures

Recommended performance indicators include:

### Risk Metrics

* Total system risk;
* Number of critical-risk assets;
* Number of high-risk assets;
* Percentage of assets above the Municipality's risk tolerance;
* Expected Annual Loss, where available.

### Investment Metrics

* Annual capital investment;
* Risk reduction achieved;
* Risk reduction per dollar invested;
* Percentage of capital directed toward high-risk assets.

### Asset Performance Metrics

* Failure frequency;
* Emergency maintenance cost;
* Service interruptions;
* Sanitary sewer overflows;
* Water main breaks;
* Unplanned equipment outages; and
* Regulatory events.

# 21. Annual Review

The capital prioritization model should be reviewed annually as part of the Municipality's budget and CIP development process.

The annual update should incorporate:

* New asset failures;
* Updated condition assessments;
* Completed projects;
* Revised project costs;
* New regulatory requirements;
* Changes in development or demand;
* Updated hydraulic modeling;
* New inspection data;
* Available funding; and
* Changes in municipal priorities.

Projects should be re-ranked using current information rather than automatically retaining their prior-year ranking.

---

# 22. Decision-Making and Engineering Judgment

Risk scores and prioritization models provide decision support. They do not independently determine which projects the Municipality should construct.

Final recommendations should consider:

* Engineering judgment;
* Operations staff experience;
* Regulatory obligations;
* Financial constraints;
* Constructability;
* Community impacts;
* Coordination opportunities;
* Uncertainty in available data; and
* Long-term municipal objectives.

Where professional judgment results in a project priority that differs materially from the calculated ranking, the basis for that decision should be documented.

This provides transparency while preserving the role of professional engineering judgment.
